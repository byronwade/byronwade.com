Software for service businesses is usually sold as a feature checklist. Operators don’t buy checklists. They buy fewer dropped balls.

I learned that running [Wade’s Plumbing and Septic](/projects/wades-plumbing-and-septic), where I grew it to **$2.4M** in year-two revenue and then lost it, before I ever started building [Thorbis](/projects/thorbis). The gaps showed up in the truck, on the phone, and in the office at 7am. Not in a pitch deck.

## Speed is a customer experience requirement

When someone’s septic is backing up, they will not wait for a heavy site or a sluggish dispatch board. Mobile page speed and one-tap contact aren’t “nice to have.” They’re the difference between winning the job and losing it to the next result.

The same applies inside the product: technicians work with one hand, bad signal, and a customer waiting. Every extra tap is a tax.

## Context has to travel with the job

Most shops stitch together SMS, email, voice, scheduling, estimates, and payments. Context scatters. The office knows something the tech doesn’t. The customer repeats the story three times. The invoice doesn’t match what was promised on the phone.

Unified communications is not a feature category. It is how you stop losing money to incomplete information.

## Trust beats glossy marketing

Homeowners are skeptical of trades for good reason. “No upsell,” real photos, family-owned framing, and honest recommendations disarm that skepticism faster than stock imagery ever will. Software that helps a business *be* trustworthy, so clear estimates and fast follow-up and reputation protection, matters more than another dashboard widget.

That’s why [Goodmarks](/projects/goodmarks) exists: route happy customers to public reviews and unhappy ones to a private inbox before a one-star rating becomes permanent.

## The phone still runs the business

Missed calls are missed revenue. Complex phone trees frustrate people who already have an emergency. AI-operated telephony and clear routing are not futurism for most shops. They are the difference between catching the job and going to voicemail.

## Measure outcomes, not features

The releases that matter tie back to operator metrics:

- Faster booking
- Higher close rates
- Fewer missed messages
- Lower days sales outstanding
- Techs who can finish paperwork in the driveway

If a feature doesn’t move one of those, it’s probably a demo feature.

## Build from the field up

The best product ideas I’ve had didn’t come from competitor matrices. They came from standing in a wet basement, juggling a phone, and realizing the tool in my hand was fighting me.

That’s the bar for service software: it should feel like it was built by somebody who has actually done the work, because the people using it can tell when it wasn’t.
